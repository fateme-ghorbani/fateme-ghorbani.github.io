Discovering Smuggling Ports and Unconventional Points of Interest

Bachelor's Thesis | Unsupervised Machine Learning | Geospatial Data Analysis

Project Overview

This project explored how Automatic Identification System (AIS) vessel data and unsupervised machine learning could help identify unconventional Points of Interest (UPOIs) and locations exhibiting potentially unusual vessel activity. The goal was to discover spatial patterns that could warrant further investigation without relying on predefined locations or labeled examples.

Data Source and Scope

The dataset was obtained from the U.S. MarineCadastre AIS dataset. The timestamps in the processed data ranged from December 31, 2020, to March 28, 2021. The study focused on a geographic area in the Pacific Ocean, bounded by longitudes -131.929° to -111.225° and latitudes 26.086° to 28.618°.

The analysis examined AIS attributes including latitude, longitude, Speed Over Ground (SOG), draft, heading, and vessel characteristics. Latitude and longitude were used as the primary inputs for spatial clustering, while vessel speed was used to focus on slow-moving observations.

Data Preprocessing

The preprocessing workflow included exploratory data analysis, inspection of missing values, handling of selected categorical attributes, and outlier detection.

  • Removed 41,051 rows identified by the combined IQR-based and rule-based outlier detection procedure.
  • Retained 263,937 rows across 17 columns after this outlier-removal step.
  • Excluded selected vessel-identification and descriptive columns from the cleaned dataset.

The outlier-removal procedure was applied across multiple numerical attributes. Consequently, the resulting dataset depended on the statistical and rule-based criteria used during preprocessing.

Problem and Challenges

A key challenge was exploring potentially meaningful geographic patterns without predefined labels or a known number of clusters. Dense groups of vessel observations may reflect ordinary maritime activity, while observations outside dense groups may warrant additional investigation. Spatial density alone, however, cannot establish whether an activity is suspicious.

Methodology: Why HDBSCAN?

I applied HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) to standardized latitude and longitude coordinates. This method was selected to explore geographic groupings without specifying the number of clusters in advance.

  • No predefined cluster count: HDBSCAN can discover clusters from the density structure of the observations.
  • Spatial pattern discovery: Density-based clustering can identify groups with irregular shapes.
  • Noise identification: Observations that are not assigned to a discovered cluster receive the noise label (-1) and can be examined separately.

The implementation used a minimum cluster size of 10 observations and focused on vessel observations with Speed Over Ground (SOG) below 5 knots. The latitude and longitude coordinates were standardized before clustering.

Results and Key Findings

  • Removed 41,051 rows during the reported outlier-removal step.
  • Retained 263,937 rows and 17 columns after outlier removal.
  • In the clustering output currently verified, HDBSCAN produced two clusters containing 32 and 13 observations, respectively, and labeled 10 observations as noise.

The verified clustering output accounts for 55 observations. This result has not yet been reconciled with the full filtered dataset, so the 10 noise observations should not be interpreted as 10 unique geographic locations or confirmed UPOIs.

Outcome and Limitations

This project explored a data-driven workflow combining AIS data preprocessing, exploratory analysis, geospatial visualization, and density-based clustering. It investigated how spatial patterns among slow-moving vessel observations could be examined to identify records for further analysis.

Important limitation: HDBSCAN noise labels indicate observations that were not assigned to a cluster; they do not establish illegal or suspicious activity. The results are exploratory and require contextual validation. The verified outputs do not currently establish the previously reported counts of 917 candidate UPOIs or zero candidates under an SOG threshold below 0.5 knots.

Through this project, I gained experience with data preprocessing, exploratory data analysis, geospatial visualization, unsupervised machine learning, and interpreting analytical findings in light of methodological limitations.
